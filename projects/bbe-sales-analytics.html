BBE Sales & Profitability Analytics

A full consultancy-style engagement: a star-schema data model, ~25 DAX measures, a 3-page executive dashboard, and a written report covering methodology, documented data limitations, and business recommendations.

The challenge

BBE, a retailer of bikes, parts, accessories, and sports apparel, needed a sales analytics solution covering product, geographic, temporal, and customer performance — built to a standard where every figure could be trusted, and every limitation in the underlying data was documented rather than hidden.

The approach

1. Understand the data before touching it

Before any cleaning, I profiled all four source tables (Products, Customers, Orders, Promotion) column by column — data types, null patterns, and referential integrity — to know exactly what I was working with.

2. Clean with a documented decision for every issue found

Every data-quality issue was logged with a finding and a decision, not silently fixed. Examples: 209 products had no category — identified as internal manufacturing components and excluded from a dedicated Products_Working table, but kept in the original query for reference. "NA" text values in Color and SizeRange were masking as real categories — replaced with true nulls. Coded fields (ProductLine, Class) were decoded to readable values, but flagged as an external assumption since the coding convention wasn't confirmed by the data itself. 24 fully duplicate order rows were removed. 5 order lines had no valid product link — kept in revenue totals but flagged and excluded from product-level breakdowns rather than dropped outright.

3. Build the star schema and ~25 DAX measures

Orders sits as the central fact table, related to three dimensions (Products_Working, Customers, Promotion) plus a custom Date table generated in Power Query and marked as the model's official date table. On top of that: ~25 measures spanning core KPIs, time intelligence (YoY, YTD, MTD, rolling averages), product ranking, and new-vs-returning customer segmentation — using the Customers table's own DateFirstPurchase field rather than recalculating it from Orders, since Orders only covers a partial window of each customer's real history.

4. Report limitations as clearly as the results

The written report includes a full Data Limitations section: the dataset ends mid-January 2020, so any period including that month is partial, not a real decline. Promotion discounts aren't reflected in the actual price charged, so promotional impact on revenue genuinely cannot be measured from this data — and the report says so plainly instead of presenting a misleading number. Shipping and due dates turned out to be fixed, invariant offsets from the order date across all 58,178 rows, meaning any "delivery time" analysis on this data would be analyzing a constant, not a real pattern — so that angle was documented as a limitation rather than presented as an insight.

Result

A three-page executive dashboard (Executive Overview, Product Performance, Customer & Geography) backed by a written consultancy report that pairs every business insight with the evidence behind it and the commercial implication — for example, that Bikes drive about 95% of revenue ($21.2M of $22.2M), that the top six best-selling products are all Mountain-200 variants, and that new customer acquisition nearly quadrupled from 2018 to 2019. Just as importantly, the report is explicit about what the data can't tell you — which is what makes the numbers it does report trustworthy.

Key business insights (from the written report)

  • Bikes generate ~95% of total revenue — a concentration risk worth flagging to management
  • The top 6 best-sellers are all one model family (Mountain-200), and the next 4 are all Road-250
  • New customer acquisition nearly quadrupled from 2018 (~3,200) to 2019 (~12,500)
  • Higher-income customers consistently spend more — a usable segmentation variable
  • The US and Australia alone account for ~60% of revenue, leaving 4 secondary markets under-tapped

What this project demonstrates

  • A full analytics workflow: business understanding → data profiling → cleaning → modeling → DAX → reporting
  • Documenting data-quality findings and decisions instead of silently patching them
  • Being explicit about what the data cannot answer, not just what it can
  • Writing for a business audience: findings paired with evidence and management implications
  • ~25 DAX measures including guarded time-intelligence and ranking patterns
