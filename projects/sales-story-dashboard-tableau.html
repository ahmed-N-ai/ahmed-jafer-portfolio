Sales Performance Story Dashboard

A one-page Tableau Public dashboard that walks the reader from the situation to the problem to the solution, following a Z-shaped reading path — built from a small sales dataset with calculated fields and table calculations.

The challenge

A practice brief asked for a dashboard built from a small sales dataset — not just charting the numbers, but leading the reader through a clear story: what is happening, why it matters, and what to do about it, readable by a manager in about ten seconds.

The approach

1. Profile the dataset first

One Excel sheet with 30 orders from January to June 2026: Date, Order ID, Region, City, Product, Category, Customer Segment, Units, Revenue — covering 3 cities (Cairo, Giza, Alexandria), 5 products (Laptop, Phone, Tablet, Desk, Chair), and 2 categories (Electronics, Furniture). The data arrived clean — I checked for missing values, duplicate rows, and inconsistent date formats during profiling and found none, so no cleaning step was needed before modeling.

2. Model with calculated fields, not a relational model

The data lives in a single flat table, so no relational model was needed. I added three calculated fields: Revenue per Unit (sum of revenue divided by sum of units), Laptop Share (Laptop units divided by all units), and a flag used only for highlighting Giza, plus percent-of-total table calculations for the city mix.

3. Analyse before designing anything

I compared units against revenue by category, city, and product before touching the layout. Prices turned out to be identical across cities — a Laptop is 8,500 in every city — so differences in revenue per unit come entirely from product mix, not pricing. I also compared each city's Laptop share against its revenue per unit.

4. Design a dashboard that tells a story

I built seven worksheets and arranged them on a fixed 1920×1080 canvas that reads in a Z shape: top-left has three KPI cards (revenue, units, revenue per unit), top-right has the monthly revenue trend with June annotated, a diagonal element bridges to the problem, bottom-left shows the problem as 100% stacked bars of units vs. revenue by city and category, and bottom-right shows the solution — revenue per unit by product against the average, plus Laptop share and revenue per unit by city. Colour carries meaning throughout: orange is Furniture, blue is Electronics.

Result

A dashboard where each chart sets up the next one: Furniture is about 52% of units but only 28% of revenue, and the cities that sell more Laptops earn more per unit. It was my first time designing the layout around a reading path instead of just placing charts.

Key insights

  • Total revenue was 940.5K from 314 units, about 2,995 per unit
  • Revenue peaked in May at 176.0K and fell to 150.8K in June; the drop came from Cairo and Giza while Alexandria rose
  • Furniture is 51.6% of units but only 28.4% of revenue
  • Giza has the lowest revenue per unit (2,692) and sold only 5 Laptops, versus 12 in Alexandria, which has the highest (3,351)
  • Cairo sold 75 Chairs — 45% of its units but only 19% of its revenue
  • One Laptop (8,500) earns as much as 6.8 Chairs (1,250)

Recommendations

Based on the data, the focus should shift toward Laptop and Phone — the only two products above the 2,995 average revenue per unit — and Giza in particular has the most room to move toward them. Two caveats: Phone is above average by a very small margin (3,000), and with only 30 orders the patterns show a direction, not a statistical rule.

What this project demonstrates

  • Designing a dashboard around a deliberate reading path (a Z shape) instead of just placing charts
  • Building calculated fields and percent-of-total table calculations in Tableau Public
  • Diagnosing a revenue gap by comparing price, product mix, and city performance before designing anything
  • Using colour with one fixed meaning throughout, not as decoration
  • Turning a handful of data points into clear, honestly-caveated business recommendations
